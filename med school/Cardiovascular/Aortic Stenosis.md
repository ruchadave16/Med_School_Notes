### Etiology
- Most common heart disease in the world
	- **Older**: degenerative calcific aortic stenosis
	- Young adults: [[congenital bicuspid aortic valve]] -- 2% of population
	- [[Rheumatic heart disease]] = huge reason globally
- Risk factors
	- Calcified: Advanced age, [[hypertension]], [[hyperlipidemia]] (elevated LDL cholesterol), diabetes, male sex, chronic kidney disease
	- Congenital:  bicuspid aortic valve

### Pathophysiology
- Narrowing or obstruction of the aortic valve opening -> ↓ ejection of blood from left ventricle during systole
- Calcified: 
	-  Endothelial damage on the aortic side of the leaflets triggers an inflammatory response 
	- Infiltration of lipids and immune cells
	- Differentiate into osteoblast-like cells that actively deposit calcium

1) Initially LV works harder -- pressure overload 
2) Concentric Hypertrophy (early)
3) Consequences (later)
	- Diastolic dysfunction: non-compliant ventricle so can't relax fully in diastole
		- ↑ end pressure
		- Pressure backs into LA and pulmonary circulation causing dyspnea
	- ↑ Oxygen demand
	- ↓ Oxygen supply: compression of subendocardial coronary arteries

4) Decompensation (late)
	- Fibrosis develops, contractility declines, and the LV may begin to dilate.
	- Causes [[chronic heart failure]]
### Clinical Features
- Long asymptomatic latent period
- **SAD Triad**
	- Syncope: on exertion, cerebral hypoperfusion
	- Angina: exertional
	- Dyspnea: exertional, earliest sign
#### Physical Exam
- Crescendo-decrescendo systolic ejection murmur
	- Right upper sternal border, often radiating to the carotid arteries
- Pulsus parvus et tardus – a weak (parvus) and delayed (tardus) carotid upstroke
- Soft or absent A2 component of the second heart sound (S2)
	- due to valve immobility
- Potential [[S4]] gallop due to forceful atrial contraction into a stiff ventricle
### Diagnosis
- TTE = gold standard
	- Visualization: Assesses valve morphology (bicuspid vs. tricuspid), leaflet thickness, calcification, and mobility
	- Doppler Measurements: Quantifies the severity of stenosis
		- Peak Velocity (Vmax): Speed of blood flow across the valve
		- Mean Pressure Gradient: Avg. pressure difference between LV and aorta
		- Aortic Valve Area (AVA): Calculated via the continuity eqn. (𝐴1𝑣1 = 𝐴2𝑣2)
	- For Severe AS: AVA ≤1.0 cm², Mean Gradient ≥40 mmHg, Vmax ≥4.0 m/s
- ECG: Might see LVH
- Chest X-ray: can show calcification

### Treatment
**always refer to the most recent AHA/ACC recommendations for guidelines**
- research into lipoprotein(a)-lowering agents and anti-inflammatory approaches (e.g., colchicine) is ongoing (Otto, et al., JAMA, 2024)

**Surgery**
- Recommended for all symptomatic with severe AS

>[!Note]
>Key Points Regarding **Mechanical vs. Bioprosthetic Valves:**
>- Valve Selection:
>	- Favor mechanical: Age 50 years, low bleeding risk, compliant with INR monitoring, other indication for anticoagulation
>	- Favor bioprosthetic: Age >65 years, high bleeding risk, limited INR access, access to valve-in-valve TAVR for future degeneration
>	- Shared decision-making is a Class I recommendation
>- Anticoagulation:
>	- Mechanical valves: Lifelong VKA (warfarin). DOACs are contraindicated.
>	- Surgical bioprosthetic valves: VKA for 3–6 months post-op (Class IIa), then aspirin 75–100 mg daily
>	- TAVR: Aspirin monotherapy (Class IIa); DAPT (aspirin + clopidogrel) for 3–6 months may be considered in low bleeding risk (Class IIb)
>	- Prosthetic valves AND AF: VKA for mechanical valves; for bioprosthetic valves, DOACs may be used based on CHA₂DS₂-VASc (except rheum. MS)

>[!Note] Age Recommnedations
>- Age 65 years (or life expectancy >20 years): SAVR recommended (Class I). TAVR not recommended due to insufficient long-term durability data.
>- Age 65–80 years: Either SAVR or transfemoral TAVR (Class I), based on shared decision-making
>- Age >80 years (or life expectancy 10 years): Transfemoral TAVR recommended over SAVR (Class I).
>- Note: ESC/EACTS guidelines use different cutoffs (75 for SAVR, >75 for TAVR)
